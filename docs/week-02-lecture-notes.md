# Week 2 facilitator notes: Surprise and information

## Teaching purpose

Week 1 established that context creates a field of more- and less-plausible continuations. Week 2 follows the field through the next step: an outcome arrives and changes it.

The central sentence for the session is:

> Information is the change an observation makes to a receiver's live field of possibilities.

Do not begin with a formula. Learners should first experience the relationship among prior expectations, an observation, and the possibilities left afterward.

## Outcomes

By the end, learners should be able to:

1. distinguish information from message length, importance, and emotional intensity;
2. explain why the same message can be informative to one receiver and routine to another;
3. compare yes-or-no questions by the branches they create before an answer is known;
4. name the source of uncertainty in several prediction tasks;
5. distinguish felt surprise from a formal information measure;
6. state why surprise offers an opportunity to learn but does not guarantee learning.

## Preparation

- Open the Week 2 page and test both interactives.
- Prepare eight object names on cards: apple, banana, carrot, hammer, mug, pillow, scissors, shoe.
- Give each learner paper with three columns: **Before**, **Observation**, **After**.
- Keep the formal expression `I(x) = -log2 p(x)` optional. It is not needed for the core lesson.

## 0-10 minutes: predict before explaining

Ask learners to rank these messages from least to most informative to the named receiver:

1. A weather app tells a Bangkok resident in August, “It is humid.”
2. A fair coin lands heads after one ordinary toss.
3. A laboratory reports that the same coin landed heads 100 times in a row.
4. A doctor tells a patient awaiting a test, “The result is negative.”
5. A stranger texts, “Yes.”

Do not settle the ranking immediately. Ask instead:

- What did the receiver consider possible before the message?
- Which item cannot be ranked without more context?
- What extra fact would change your ranking?

The important discovery is that “Yes” has no fixed information value without a question and a receiver. The medical result also invites legitimate disagreement because learners may imagine different prior evidence. Use that disagreement rather than resolving it prematurely.

## 10-20 minutes: two messages

Display:

> The sun rose this morning.

> Your bank called about a large transfer.

Have learners fill the three columns before discussing.

| Before | Observation | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What outcomes were seriously possible? | What arrived? | Which possibilities disappeared or became less plausible? |

Then change only the receiver:

- the account owner;
- the employee already reviewing the transfer;
- a stranger who received the message by mistake.

Suggested talk track:

> The words did not change, but the receiver's starting point did. Information is therefore not a substance stored in a sentence. It describes how an observation changes a specified field of possibilities.

Avoid saying the sunrise message contains “zero information.” Under ordinary conditions it carries very little new information; under an unusual condition—an astronaut awaiting confirmation, for example—the same sentence may matter.

## 20-45 minutes: Twenty Questions

First play with the eight physical cards. One learner secretly chooses an item. Before each question, the group must record:

- the predicted size of the **yes** branch;
- the predicted size of the **no** branch;
- which answer they expect;
- how many candidates each answer would leave.

Require three questions:

1. a question producing a very uneven split;
2. a question producing the most even split the group can find;
3. a relevant-sounding question that eliminates nothing.

Then use the website's question-splitter and compare its counts with the group's predictions.

### Debrief

Ask:

- Which question was useful even before we knew its answer?
- Why does “Is it the television?” usually achieve little?
- Did an even split guarantee that the wording was sensible?
- When might an uneven question still be worth asking?

Suggested talk track:

> Evaluate a question prospectively: inspect the branches before the answer arrives. A near-even split often works because either answer removes a substantial part of the field. It is a heuristic, not a universal rule. Cost, reliability, time, and unequal consequences also matter.

Use a medical test as the boundary case. A dangerous condition may be unlikely, yet testing for it can still be worthwhile because missing it has a high cost.

## 45-65 minutes: prediction-difficulty ladder

Learners rank the six events in the website activity. For every event, require the sentence:

> My uncertainty comes mainly from ______.

Collect answers under these headings:

- fixed text or rehearsed structure;
- contextual constraint;
- limited personal knowledge;
- hidden or changing causes;
- chaotic dynamics;
- deliberate randomness;
- many equally plausible alternatives.

The rank is less important than the diagnosis. “Hard to predict” is not one mechanism. A coin flip and tomorrow's weather can both be difficult for very different reasons.

## 65-80 minutes: misconception clinic

Assign each pair one claim:

- Longer messages contain more information.
- Rare events are always useful.
- Feeling shocked is the same as receiving information.
- Every unexpected event should change your belief.
- Uncertainty is merely ignorance.
- The best question always divides the options exactly in half.

Each pair must provide:

1. a counterexample;
2. a repaired version without *always*, *only*, *really*, or *just*;
3. one condition under which the repaired claim stops working.

Useful corrections:

- Message length and information can vary independently.
- A rare observation may carry high formal surprise under a model without being relevant or useful for a decision.
- Felt shock also depends on attention, emotion, memory, and stakes.
- An unexpected event may be noise or come from an unreliable source.
- Uncertainty can describe a genuinely random process, not merely missing knowledge.
- Balanced branches are one consideration among cost, consequence, and reliability.

## 80-90 minutes: set up the surprise journal

Learners record three small surprises during the following week. For each:

1. What happened?
2. What did I expect instead?
3. What evidence supported that expectation?
4. What changed afterward: my belief, my model, or only my mood?
5. If nothing changed, why not?

Say explicitly:

> Surprise is evidence of a mismatch between an expectation and an observation. It creates an opportunity to learn. Whether and how much to update depends on the quality of the evidence.

## 90-100 minutes: behavioral checkpoint

Learners create three original examples:

1. a long message carrying very little information to its receiver;
2. a one-word message carrying a great deal;
3. a relevant-sounding yes-or-no question that barely narrows the possibilities.

For every example, they must name:

- the receiver;
- the live possibilities before the message or answer;
- what changed afterward.

The response passes when the explanation depends on prior expectations rather than message length or emotional drama.

## Common teaching mistakes

### Explaining before learners predict

Collect rankings and branch predictions first. The lesson is about changes in expectation, so learners need a visible prior expectation of their own.

### Treating information as usefulness or truth

A message can be surprising but false, irrelevant, or harmful. Keep separate questions separate: How unexpected was it? Was it reliable? Was it useful for this decision?

### Treating an even split as the definition of a good question

It is a clean classroom heuristic for reducing a possibility set. Real decisions also include costs, risks, time, and asymmetric consequences.

### Equating a feeling with a technical quantity

Felt surprise is an accessible entry point. Formal self-information requires a stated probability model and does not require a person to feel anything.

### Saying “no surprise, no learning” without qualification

Expected practice can strengthen a skill, and many tiny discrepancies can accumulate without dramatic surprise. The narrower defensible claim is that evidence already guaranteed by a model gives that model no reason, by itself, to redistribute belief among alternatives.

## Optional technical extension

For learners ready for notation, introduce:

`I(x) = -log2 p(x)`

Use it only to make three points:

1. lower-probability outcomes carry more self-information under the stated model;
2. the value changes when the probability model changes;
3. high self-information does not automatically mean high practical value.

Return immediately to a concrete contrast: one fair coin toss versus 100 reported heads in a row. Do not turn the session into a logarithm lesson.
