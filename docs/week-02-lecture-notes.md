# Week 2 facilitator notes: Surprise and information

## Purpose

Connect Week 1's guesses to what happens when an answer arrives. Use three questions throughout:

> What did you think could happen? What did the message say? What do you now know?

The lesson distinguishes information from message length, usefulness, and emotional surprise. No formula is required.

## Preparation

Open Week 2 and its two widgets. Prepare paper with **Before / Message / After** columns. For a physical question game, use eight cards: apple, banana, carrot, hammer, mug, pillow, scissors, shoe.

The timings below are a suggested two-hour session, not measured completion times. Solo learners can pause between activities.

## 0–15 minutes: same message, different knowledge

A parcel may arrive Monday or Tuesday. You do not know which; your neighbor already saw the schedule. Both receive “Tuesday.”

Ask each learner to fill the three columns for both people. The words stay the same; what each person learns differs.

Then try “Heads” after a fair coin toss and “Yes” with no question attached. Ask what context is missing. Avoid ranking messages by how dramatic they sound.

## 15–45 minutes: choose a useful question

Choose one of eight cards with equal probability. Before each yes-or-no question, count how many items each answer would leave.

Compare:

- A 4/4 split: either answer leaves four.
- A 1/7 split: one answer leaves one, but it occurs only one time in eight; the other leaves seven.

The even split leaves fewer items on average under these assumptions. An unlikely “yes” to the 1/7 question can still be very informative once received. Distinguish choosing a question beforehand from evaluating one answer afterward.

Repeat in the widget, counting its actual items. Do not assume the widget uses the same eight-card set.

If learners ask about unequal probabilities, use a simple variation: one item was chosen in most past rounds. Asking about that item first may be useful. Equal item counts are not equal probabilities in every task.

## 45–65 minutes: why is a prediction hard?

Use the difficulty ladder. Accept different orders when learners explain their assumptions.

Ask for plain reasons:

- “I remember the fixed wording.”
- “The sentence gives strong clues.”
- “I lack information about this person.”
- “Several outcomes are possible.”
- “We are treating the choice as random.”

For the fair coin, assume no clue about the outcome beyond its equal chances. Do not turn that teaching assumption into a universal claim that physical measurements could never help predict a toss.

Save the ranking for Week 8's comparison of text continuations.

## 65–85 minutes: repair one claim

Choose one:

- Longer messages always tell us more.
- Surprising messages are always useful.
- The best question always divides the options in half.

Each learner gives a counterexample, then a more accurate one-sentence version.

Example: a repeated timetable may be long but tell its reader nothing new; a one-word change to the arrival day may matter. Keep the receiver's starting knowledge explicit.

## 85–100 minutes: name the ideas

**Information:** what a message tells a receiver depends on what they already knew.

**Surprise:** an outcome is more surprising under an expectation that gave it a lower chance. Emotional shock also depends on stakes and attention.

**Learning:** a surprising event can prompt a revision, but one event does not always justify it. A late bus could be an exception rather than a new timetable.

For facilitators: the course uses this intuition before formal definitions. Do not equate every change of belief with a guaranteed decrease in uncertainty. If a formal question arises, distinguish event surprisal from expected uncertainty; defer calculations to further study. Week 10 introduces text-prediction loss, not a full information-theory unit.

## 100–120 minutes: checkpoint and next step

Ask for a long low-information message, a short high-information message, and a yes-or-no question whose likely answer removes few options. Learners should state the prior knowledge in each case.

A satisfactory answer depends on expectations and possibilities, not just message length or emotional intensity.

Between sessions, record three surprises: expectation, event, and whether the evidence justifies a changed expectation. Next week asks which details a useful model should keep.
